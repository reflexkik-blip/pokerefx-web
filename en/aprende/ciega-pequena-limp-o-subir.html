Small blind: limp or raise? How to play the SB in tournaments

When to limp, raise or go all-in from the small blind in poker tournaments, how the BB should attack your limp and what to do if you are raised (limp-call, limp-fold, limp-shove). With solver data.

The small blind is the strangest position in poker: you already have half a blind in, only one opponent is left and they have position on you. That is why the modern equilibrium plays lots of hands by limping and keeps raising for part of the range.

“BB attacks the limp” is how often the BB raises (iso-raise) or goes all-in against your limp. “SB folds to the attack” is how often the SB folds after limping and facing the raise.

Conclusion: the raise size hardly matters (2.5, 3 or 3.5 bb give practically the same EV if you keep the limps), but giving up the limp does cost: about 0.01-0.03 bb per hand with 10-12 big blinds (there almost everything is all-in or fold anyway) and between 0.05 and 0.11 bb per hand from 14 big blinds up. If you find limped pots hard to play, raising 3.5 bb is a playable simplification, but the strategy with limps wins more.

When the BB raises you after the limp

  • Limp-fold: weak hands that came in for the price.
  • Limp-call: playable hands that don't want to risk the whole stack.
  • Limp-shove (limp-raise all-in): with 25 bb or fewer, the strongest limping hands and some bluffs with blockers.

Frequently asked questions

Is limping from the small blind good?

Yes: the tournament equilibrium limps more than half of its hands from the SB at almost every depth, and raises or goes all-in with the rest of its range.

What is a limp-shove?

Limping from the SB and, when the BB raises, going all-in. It is used with 25 big blinds or fewer with strong hands and some bluffs.
